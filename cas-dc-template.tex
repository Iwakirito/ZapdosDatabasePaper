\begin{abstract}
The development of solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) is increasingly driven by data-centric screening and machine-learning approaches, yet the experimental conductivity record remains difficult to reuse at scale because it is fragmented and often not machine-linkable to a specific crystal structure. Generalist databases such as the Materials Project (MP) provide curated structures and computed descriptors, but typically lack experimentally measured ionic conductivities. Conversely, specialist SSE datasets curate conductivity measurements but frequently provide only coarse or inconsistent structural descriptors (\textit{e.g.} family labels, space group), leaving it ambiguous which polymorph a reported conductivity pertains to. This structural ambiguity limits reproducible comparison across studies, complicates polymorph-aware interpretation of transport trends, obstructs direct joining to computed descriptors, hinders rigorous theory-experiment benchmarking, and degrades the reliability of structure-aware data models.

Here, we address this bottleneck by harmonizing three open experimental SSE conductivity datasets against MP, creating a merged corpus in which each retained conductivity measurement is explicitly linked to a unique MP identifier and annotated with a tiered, transparent confidence label. After cleaning and normalizing compositions and conductivity values, the resulting dataset comprises 3123 measurements mapped onto 310 distinct MP entries. Of these, 554 entries fall into high-confidence tiers (T1--T3), while a broader set of lower-confidence matches (T4) is retained to preserve chemical coverage. The tiering scheme exposes match reliability and enables users to select precision-focused or coverage-focused subsets without conflating the two.

A statistical analysis shows that the structure-assigned subset is dominated by oxide and sulfide families central to contemporary SSE research, reproduces expected conductivity trends across structural families, and quantifies key limitations, including sparse temperature metadata and limited per-material coverage. Finally, we demonstrate a simple retrieval-augmented query interface combining semantic search with deterministic ranking to explore the conductivity landscape in a numerically grounded manner. Overall, we deliver a reusable, structure-anchored SSE conductivity dataset and a generalizable blueprint for converting experimentally reported properties into interoperable, machine-readable links to large-scale materials repositories.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Interest in solid-electrolytes (SEs) dates back approximately 200 years to the work of Michael Faraday, with a period of rapid development in the 1960s \cite{zhao2020designing}. In the last decade, SEs have re-emerged as a materials class of notable scientific and commercial interest, standing at the heart of all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs) \cite{famprikis2019fundamentals}. These emerging electrochemical energy storage systems promise advantages over conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), including enhanced safety due to the non-flammable nature of the SEs, as well as the potential for higher energy and power densities \cite{zhao2020designing, janek2023challenges}. The shift to ASSBs is considered a realistic advancement, with theoretical calculations suggesting a potential specific energy of up to $393 \text{ Wh } \text{kg}^{-1}$ and an energy density of $1,143 \text{ Wh } \text{L}^{-1}$ \cite{janek2023challenges}. However, the core challenge hindering widespread commercial viability is the limited ionic conductivity of many SEs at room temperature (RT), a property that governs charge and discharge rates \cite{zhao2020designing, therrien2025obelix}. While some sulfide-based conductors have achieved ionic conductivities comparable to liquid electrolytes (in the order of $1 \text{ mS } \text{cm}^{-1}$), the discovery of materials with the full suite of required properties, including sufficiently high ionic conductivity, stability against its alkali metal, and suitable mechanical properties, requires a dramatic shift from traditional trial-and-error methods \cite{therrien2025obelix, zhao2020designing, yu2025data}. The high complexity of the materials space makes the exploration and screening of high-performance SEs difficult, as conventional experimental methods and theoretical computations for exhaustive material search are time- and resource-consuming \cite{yang2024dynamic, mishra2023exploring}.

The increasing relevance of SEs in literature is matched by a corresponding demand for robust, machine-readable databases capable of supporting high-throughput and data-driven approaches \cite{yang2024dynamic}, enabling standardized workflows and persistent identifiers that will unlock automated querying, cross-database integration, and provenance tracking. The Materials Genome Initiative (MGI), launched in 2011, endorsed the ambition to computationally predict the existence and basic properties of essentially every single material \cite{armiento2020database}. The resulting trend integrates computational materials science with information technology, spurring the development of resources such as the Materials Project \cite{armiento2020database, jain2013commentary}. Databases are a necessary consequence of modern research, which provides large amounts of digital data that require homogeneous and coherent organization for efficient computer processing and new scientific inferences \cite{grazulis2012crystallography}. Despite this shift, the existing data landscape for solid electrolytes is severely fragmented and inconsistent. Data on critical properties like ionic conductivity are scattered across different scientific papers, and materials frequently lack standardized reporting or unique identifiers. A central challenge in data utilization is the ambiguity concerning the material's identity: a single stoichiometric formula can represent multiple distinct crystal structures, each with drastically different electrochemical behavior. Furthermore, literature reports often use different conventions or omit essential metadata. This fragmentation and lack of standardization make it exceedingly difficult to combine data from different sources for comprehensive analysis. More broadly, this reflects a well-recognized challenge in experimental materials data: the lack of standardized, machine-readable identifiers and annotations that enable unambiguous cross-study and cross-database linking.

In the materials science domain, the database ecosystem can be broadly divided into generalist and specific platforms. Generalist databases, such as the widely-used, open-access Materials Project (MP), the Crystallography Open Database (COD), and the proprietary Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD), offer vast collections of structural and computed material properties \cite{armiento2020database}. The Materials Project, for instance, provides an open dataset generated via high-throughput computing to uncover the properties of inorganic materials \cite{jain2013commentary}, currently holding computed data for more than 70,000 materials and millions of associated properties \cite{ye2018harnessing}, and offering a high-level of uniformity and versatility. However, these generalist platforms often do not prioritize, or systematically include, the key experimentally verified solid-state electrolyte properties, such as ionic conductivity, that are crucial for materials screening. Conversely, specific SSE databases, like the Dynamic Database of Solid-State Electrolytes (DDSE) \cite{yang2024dynamic}, the Liverpool Ionics (LiIon) Dataset \cite{hargreaves2023database}, the OBELiX dataset \cite{therrien2025obelix}, and the dataset from Shon et al. (2023) \cite{shon2023extracting}, focus on gathering these relevant experimental properties. While invaluable, these databases are typically smaller, lack uniformity, and do not provide the wealth of pre-calculated theoretical and structural descriptors found in generalist platforms. For instance, many experimental SSE databases contain limited structural information: the DDSE and Shon and Min databases contain only a qualitative structure description for some materials, the LiIon dataset only includes the structural family, and the Laskowski dataset is limited to space group information, making it difficult to train ML models that require a full crystal description \cite{therrien2025obelix}. A high-level comparison of these database types is provided in Table \ref{tab:database_comparison} below.

This scenario of mislabeled or ambiguous data, where a chemical composition may correspond to different phases or doped variants without addressing its underlying crystal structure (\textit{e.g.} lattice parameters, space group, and even atomic positions or partial occupancy) \cite{therrien2025obelix, mishra2023exploring} ends up undermining data-intensive research that often depends on datasets that are not only large, but also structured, curated, and machine-readable, with explicit identifiers and minimal contextual metadata. Such infrastructure is required for information retrieval, systematic comparison across studies, reproducible meta-analysis, theory-experiment benchmarking, and, most recently, to enable natural-language, query driven access to materials data through retrieval-based AI assistants, leveraging data-driving materials research and supporting large-scale screening, hypothesis generation \cite{ye2018harnessing} while answers still remains traceable to specific records and sources. 

Another emerging use of such curated, machine-readable dataset with unambiguous material identifiers is machine learning (ML) \cite{hargreaves2023database}, that can both be used as a promising tool to accelerate candidate prioritization in high-throughput screening workflows \cite{yang2024dynamic}, but also for property prediction, aiming to learn structure-property relationships and therefore estimate properties that are critical for the device performance - such as ionic conductivity \cite{yu2025data, mishra2023exploring}.

Under this context, the model's ability to capture the relevant variability and generalize within a given materials family tends to be benefited by providing higher-fidelity inputs that are mechanistically correlated with ion transport (\textit{e.g.} local coordination environments, diffusion-channel topology, site connectivity, and lattice metrics) \cite{hargreaves2023database}. However, the fundamental, generalist databases that are able to provide such fundamental properties are usually disconnected to the key experimentally verified solid-state electrolyte values of ionic conductivity, diffusion, and other properties that are crucial for materials screening. On another hand, the manual and expert-driven process required to collect and curate high-quality experimental data is complex and time-consuming, resulting in datasets that are typically very small for ML standards (often fewer than 10,000 entries) \cite{hargreaves2023database}, highlighting the necessity for new, densely-featured datasets that systematically links fundamental properties to experimentally measured transport parameters for a well defined, labeled material structure \cite{therrien2025obelix} . 

In this work, we address this gap by harmonizing three open-access datasets of experimentally reported solid-state electrolytes (OBELiX, LiIon, and the Shon et al. (2023) \cite{shon2023extracting}) within a large-scale identification workflow that links experimental transport properties to standardized structural and theoretical descriptors from the Materials Project. Beyond resolving which specific material each record refers to, this linkage enables the systematic reuse of high-quality computed descriptors alongside experimentally measured performance. To explicitly capture uncertainty arising from polymorphism, doping, and inconsistent reporting, we introduce a tier system that quantifies the reliability of formula-to-structure assignments. The resulting resource supports both broad exploratory analyses and high-confidence subsets tailored to downstream tasks such as benchmarking, model development, and screening.

Finally, we develop and assess a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) interface on top of the unified database to enable evidence-grounded, natural-language querying of the curated records. The interface is designed to surface and summarize results directly from retrieved database entries, preserving traceability to specific records and sources. By using representative SSE queries, we show that this approach can efficiently retrieve chemically coherent subsets and produce numerically consistent summaries, while also exposing practical limitations such as the need to explicitly define project-specific labels (\textit{e.g.}, tier semantics) to avoid misinterpretation. 

\section{Data Processing and Cleaning}

The methodology for constructing the harmonized solid-state electrolyte (SSE) database proceeds in two sequential stages: (i) the unification of heterogeneous experimental sources into a common schema, and (ii) the systematic cleaning and normalization of the resulting entries. A high-level overview of this pipeline is provided as pseudocode in Algorithm \ref{alg:harmonization}, serving as a concise reference for the detailed description that follows.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{SSE Database Harmonization and Cleaning}
\label{alg:harmonization}

\tcp{Stage 1: Harmonization}
\ForEach{database $\in$ \{OBELiX, Liverpool\_Ionics, Shon\_2023\}}{
    \ForEach{entry in database}{
        Map entry to unified schema (Specific ID, Conductivity, DOI, Temperature, Space Group, CIF, ICSD Link)
    }
}

\tcp{Stage 2: Space Group Inference}
\ForEach{entry with missing space group}{
    \eIf{entry is amorphous}{
        Exclude from Materials Project matching
    }{
        Infer candidate space group(s) from family cluster
    }
}

\tcp{Stage 3: Formula Cleaning}
\ForEach{entry}{
    \If{formula is undefined or too generic}{Exclude entry}
}

\tcp{Stage 4: Conductivity Normalization}
\ForEach{remaining entry}{
    \If{conductivity is a range or has notation errors}{
        Parse and convert to single Float64 value via logarithmic average
    }
}
\end{algorithm}

The first step of the proposed methodology is to harmonize the experimental solid-state electrolyte (SSE) databases (\textit{i.e.} OBELiX \cite{therrien2025obelix}, the Liverpool Ionics Dataset \cite{durdy2023liverpool, hargreaves2023database} and the dataset from Shon et al. (2023) \cite{shon2023extracting}). This mapping step consisted of parsing the original tables (CSV/JSON) and, when required, extracting values from accompanying metadata fields so that all entries can be treated uniformly in downstream identification and matching. 
In order to preserve provenance, each raw entry is mapped onto a common schema composed by seven fields: (1) a source-specific identifier ("Specific ID") to maintain traceability to the originating database, (2) ionic conductivity, (3) the DOI of the primary reference, (4) measurement temperature, (5) the reported space group when available, (6) the crystal structure file (CIF) or the minimal structural information required to generate it, and (7) any pre-existing link to a generalist identifier (ICSD), when the source database already provides one. The “Specific ID” was constructed using the format [XXX]–[Original ID], where [XXX] is a three-letter prefix denoting the source database (e.g., OBX, LIV, ACS) and [Original ID] is the identifier used in that database. For instance, the OBELiX entry with identifier "\textit{ywe}" was assigned the Specific ID "OBX–ywe". This convention ensures globally unique identifiers across merged datasets while preserving traceability to the originating source. All temperature values were standardized to °C. When temperature or structural descriptors were unavailable, the corresponding fields were recorded as missing values and addressed during subsequent cleaning and matching steps.

Given the frequent absence of a definitive space group in the experimental databases, \textit{i.e.} item (5) from the harmonized seven-property structure, other supplementary information such as family clusters (\textit{e.g.}, Garnet, Perovskite, NASICON) were taken into consideration to infer the most probable space group (SG) or a manageable subgroup of possibilities according to established crystallographic knowledge for each structural family. For instance, materials identified as belonging to the high-symmetry Garnet family were primarily assumed to possess the space group $Ia\bar{3}d (230)$, while the NASICON family allowed for a defined range of SGs, including $R\bar{3}c (167)$, $R\bar{3} (148)$, or $C2/c (15)$, which served as crucial filtering criteria in the subsequent matching phase. This structural inference was only applied to presumed crystalline materials; entries categorized as "Glass" or "Glass-Ceramic," which are amorphous, were noted as not having a presence in the crystalline-focused Materials Project and were consequently excluded from the MP matching phase. The full set of assumptions regarding family clusters and their likely space groups is summarized in Table \ref{tab:family_clusters_sg}.

The second stage of cleaning addressed internal inconsistencies and format errors within the raw data columns, with primary focus on the chemical formula and the conductivity values. Entries that lacked a proper chemical formula, such as those only named "Polyethylene oxide" or generalized names like "NASICON," were classified as Underdefined Formulae and not considered. This exclusion was critical because while a "classic" formula exists for NASICON ($\text{Na}_{1+x}\text{Zr}_2\text{Si}_x\text{P}_{3-x}\text{O}_{12}$), the term often refers to numerous doped variants in literature, and without further verification, automated matching is unreliable. Similarly, highly inconsistent reporting styles for conductivity values were identified and addressed. String-based values had to be programmatically converted to a unified numerical form (\textit{i.e.} \textit{Float64}). Simple notational errors, such as a leading dash in cases like $-10^{-10}$, were resolved by removing the dash and interpreting the value as $10^{-10}$. Entries reported with operators as strings (\textit{e.g.}, "$1.0 \times 10^{-7}$") were replaced by the numerical multiplicative operator and also converted to a \textit{Float64} type. For values representing ranges or averages (e.g., "2.6 $\times 10^{-3}$ and 1.4 $\times 10^{-3}$" or "0.154 to 0.045"), the logarithmic average of the endpoints was computed to generate a single representative value. In cases where only a maximum bound was given (\textit{e.g.}, "to $1.8 \times 10^{-4}$"), that value was adopted directly. Furthermore, noisy or garbled entries, such as "$10^{-5}-1 \times 10^{-6}$" (80 cases which required manual DOI checking to confirm the authors intended "$10^{-5} \sim 10^{-6}$"), were resolved by calculating the logarithmic average. The goal of this multi-stage cleaning and normalization process, schematically summarized in Figure \ref{fig:datacleaning}, was to ensure that every remaining entry possessed a verifiable composition and a singular, standardized numerical value for ionic conductivity, making the data fit for high-throughput matching and predictive modeling.

\section{Matching Methodology and Tiers}

To unify the cleaned experimental records with Materials Project (MP) entries, we define a hierarchical matching methodology composed of successive stages of increasing generality (Figure \ref{fig:hierarch}). The goal is not only to assign an MP identifier (MP-ID) to each experimental entry, but also to explicitly document which type of evidence supports that linkage. For this reason, each match is labeled with a \textit{Tier}, where tiers represent an evidence hierarchy based on the information used to establish correspondence: direct identifier provenance, structure-level agreement, or symmetry-constrained formula matching. Lower-numbered tiers correspond to stronger, more direct evidence (\textit{e.g.}, database cross-references or crystallographic equivalence), whereas higher-numbered tiers indicate matches supported by progressively weaker constraints and therefore higher ambiguity. This tier annotation enables downstream users to select subsets appropriate to their task - for example, prioritizing strict tiers for benchmarking and model training, or including broader tiers for exploratory screening and hypothesis generation. Tiers should therefore be interpreted as an evidence ranking rather than a guarantee of uniqueness, particularly for compositions with multiple polymorphs.

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figs/hierarch.png}
    \caption{Hierarchical Matching Methodology and Confidence Tier Assignment.}
    \label{fig:hierarch}
\end{figure*}

Tier~0 (Direct Identifier Provenance) attempts to establish an MP linkage using explicit, source-provided cross-references that can be resolved directly in MP, prior to any structure- or symmetry-based inference. Two identifier routes are supported. First, when an ICSD accession number is available, the entry is queried using \texttt{MaterialsProjectAPI.by\_icsd}, and a Tier~0 match is assigned only if the ICSD value reported by the source record exactly coincides with an \texttt{database\_IDs.icsd} entry returned by MP, yielding a direct mapping to an MP material identifier (MP-ID). Second, when a DOI is available, we query MP using \texttt{mpr.doi.search()} to retrieve MP entries associated with that publication; a Tier~0 match is assigned only when the DOI resolves to an unambiguous MP-ID (\textit{i.e.}, a single candidate after query and filtering), while non-unique DOI returns are treated as insufficient for direct provenance and are forwarded to subsequent tiers. If neither identifier route yields an unambiguous linkage, the record proceeds to the structure- and symmetry-based stages (Tiers~1-4). In the experimental sources considered here, this identifier-based stage did not yield successful matches; it is included for completeness and generality of the methodology, and the effective linkage is therefore achieved by the subsequent tiers.

%The algorithm's execution begins with the Direct Identifier Provenance (Tier 0). This primary matching criterion employs the presence of the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database (ICSD) accession number, as a direct provenance link. For each entry, the corresponding identifier is programmatically queried against the Materials Project using the specific routine, MaterialsProjectAPI.by\_icsd. This function interrogates the MP summary documentation and compares the database\_IDs.icsd field of each returned document against the source identifier. An exact numerical match is considered a definitive link between the datasets. Tier 0 is consequently destined for entries that presented a ICSD that could directly lead to an MP-ID, or else, that presented a DOI that was related to a specific MP-ID.

Tier~1 (Geometric Isomorphism) is applied to records that do not yield an unambiguous identifier-based linkage in Tier~0 but provide crystallographic structure information in the form of a CIF. This tier is evaluated after Tier~0 because structure matching necessarily relies on numerical tolerances (\textit{e.g.}, small variations in refined atomic positions, lattice parameters, or reported occupancies across sources), whereas identifier provenance provides a discrete and exact linkage when available. For Tier~1, candidate MP structures associated with the entry are retrieved using \texttt{MaterialsProjectAPI.structures\_for} and compared against the experimental CIF by converting both to \texttt{pymatgen.Structure} objects and evaluating geometric equivalence with \texttt{StructureMatcher}. Unless otherwise stated, we use $ltol=0.2$, $stol=0.3$, and $angle\_tol=5^{\circ}$, such that two structures are considered isomorphic when they match within these tolerances. When a match is found, the entry is assigned to Tier~1 and the corresponding CIF reference is retained as provenance, providing structure-level evidence of correspondence that is independent of space-group labeling.

%For entries failing the Tier~0, the second stage implements a Geometric Isomorphism Test (Tier 1). This requires the availability of the entry's Crystal Information File (CIF). Candidate MP structures are retrieved via the MaterialsProjectAPI.structures\_for command and compared directly against the experimental entry's CIF file. Both structures are parsed into pymatgen.Structure objects and rigorously evaluated using the StructureMatcher algorithm, also part of the Pymatgen library. A tiered tolerance-based approach is utilized to assess isomorphism: the StructureMatcher is applied with a site tolerance ($ltol$) of $0.2$, a stoichiometric tolerance ($stol$) of $0.3$, and an angle tolerance ($angle\_tol$) of $5^{\circ}$. This stage provides auditable crystallographic equivalence independent of symmetry labeling, with the specific CIF path recorded as provenance. Tier 1 is destined for entries that were matched through this CIF comparison, meaning the geometry presented in the specific database was correspondent to the geometry presented in the Materials Project Database.

%To ensure that the composition was deterministically identifiable regardless of the source database’s input ordering (\textit{e.g.}, \ce{Li6La2CaNb2O12} vs. \ce{Li6CaLa2Nb2O12}, the Python Materials Genomics (pymatgen) library \cite{ong2013python} was integrated into the pipeline. Specifically, the pipeline invoked the normalize\_formula function to instantiate a pymatgen.core.Composition object and emit its reduced\_formula, guaranteeing a deterministic, alphabetized stoichiometry (\textit{e.g.}, \ce{CaLa2Li6Nb2O12}).

The final stage performs \emph{formula-based matching with symmetry filtering} (Tiers~2--4) for records that do not yield an unambiguous identifier-based linkage (Tier~0) and for which structure-level comparison is not possible (Tier~1; \textit{e.g.}, missing CIF). Following the evidence-tier definition introduced above, these tiers assign MP candidates using progressively weaker constraints based on composition and symmetry. First, each composition is parsed into a \texttt{pymatgen.core.Composition} object and converted to a canonical reduced representation using \texttt{Composition(formula).reduced\_formula} \cite{ong2013python}. The reduced formula is then used to retrieve all compositionally identical MP candidates via \texttt{MaterialsProjectAPI.by\_formula}, generating a candidate pool that is subsequently filtered using symmetry information (including inferred space groups from Table~\ref{tab:family_clusters_sg} when applicable).

Tier~2 is assigned when the experimental entry and at least one MP candidate share the same space-group symbol after normalization (whitespace removal and hyphen standardization). This criterion applies a strict symmetry constraint beyond composition and therefore provides stronger match evidence than the subsequent tiers. If no exact space-group agreement is found, a broader symmetry constraint is applied at the crystal-system level. The crystal system (\textit{e.g}., triclinic, monoclinic, orthorhombic) is inferred from the space-group number using a standard lookup routine (\textit{e.g.}, \texttt{crystal\_system\_from\_number}). Tier~3 is assigned when the experimental entry and an MP candidate share the same crystal system, capturing cases where space-group reporting differs while remaining within the same lattice family.

Finally, entries that yield compositionally identical MP candidates (\textit{i.e.} same chemical formula) but for which the available symmetry information cannot be used to support the linkage (\textit{e.g.}, missing, inconsistent, or discordant even at the crystal-system level) are assigned Tier~4. This tier represents the least constrained linkage within the hierarchy and therefore the highest ambiguity among matches produced by the formula-based stage. This hierarchical methodology therefore combines identifier provenance, geometry-based validation, and symmetry-constrained compositional search to assign MP linkages with explicitly stated match evidence, enabling users to select subsets aligned with their tolerance for ambiguity in downstream analyses.

    %\item Lattice System Match (Tier 3): If no exact space-group match was found, a broader comparison at the crystal system level was performed. \section{Database Implementation into an AI-assisted structure}
This case study introduces a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) interface. This interface enables users to access curated conductivity records in natural language, with evidence always supported by direct references (provenance) to exact database entries. All results can be traced back to the source database entries. The following subsections describe how each stage was implemented. Figure \ref{fig:CaseStudyGraphicalAbstract} gives a visual summary. 



We evaluate the interface using sample queries about SSEs to highlight its strengths and limitations for this dataset. \subsection{Methodology}
\subsubsection{Documents Generation and Embedding}

To enable semantic querying over the matched conductivity database, we serialized each row of the curated dataset into a self-contained textual document. Each document consists of standardized column names paired with their corresponding values, with missing or undefined entries represented by the literal token \textit{NA}. To avoid exceeding a practical context length, documents longer than 4000 characters were truncated with an appended ellipsis. A canonical identifier (ID) was assigned to each entry to enable traceability back to the source database.



Finally, each document was embedded using the \textit{Sentence Transformers}\textit{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} encoder \cite{reimers2019sentence}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2}}, mapping documents to a 384-dimensional dense representation. Embeddings were L2-normalized (unit length) to make cosine similarity equivalent to inner product. The resulting matrix of shape N x 384 was converted to 32-bit floating-point before indexing.

For similarity search, we employed FAISS \cite{johnson2019billion, douze2024faiss} with an exact inner-product index (IndexFlatIP), using the embedding size as the index dimensionality. Given the modest dataset size, the exact k-nearest-neighbor search provides deterministic, fast retrieval without the approximation errors introduced by more complex indexing schemes (\textit{e.g.} product quantization or HNSW).

The embedding pipeline produces three persistent artifacts: the FAISS index, the serialized passages, and a metadata record capturing provenance information (model name, column mappings, and a cryptographic hash of the source file). These artifacts are immutable and produced once per dataset version. All subsequent queries operate exclusively on them, ensuring reproducibility across a fixed version of the underlying database.  This system is graphically represented in Figure \ref{fig:CardsCreation}.

\subsubsection{Retrieval-augmented query interface}
%The retrieval interface uses two hyperparameters: the number of retrieved neighbors \textit{k} and the number of top-ranked records t\textit{top\textunderscore n} . Typical values in this case study are $k \in [30, 60]$  and $top$\textunderscore n $\in [5,30]$, as detailed in the Supplementary Information.

The previously described text-vector index is queried via a lightweight retrieval-augmented interface. This interface takes a free-text question and retrieves a semantically relevant subset of database entries. It then applies a deterministic numerical ranking over that subset. Finally, it uses a local LLM to produce a short answer grounded in real experimental data.



The retrieval uses two key hyperparameters: number of retrieved neighbors \textit{k} and the number of top-ranked records t\textit{top\textunderscore n} . Typical values in this case study are $k \in [30, 60]$  and $top$\textunderscore n $\in [5,30]$, as detailed in the Supplementary Information.



For semantic retrieval, each incoming question is embedded into the same vector space used during index construction through the Sentence Transformers. The resulting 384-dimensional vector is L2-normalized and then passed to FAISS. FAISS performs an exact k-nearest-neighbor search under inner product (cosine) similarity. The service requests the top-k indices and maps them back to the corresponding text document. This is the only semantic step in the pipeline. All further processing uses explicit fields from these retrieved cards.

To answer conductivity-focused questions, the interface does not delegate any numeric reasoning to the language model. Instead, it uses a deterministic post-retrieval ranking of the retrieved subset. Simple regular-expression matchers extract fields of interest from each document. Documents are sorted by conductivity in descending order, and the top-N results are presented to the language model. This deterministic ranking ensures the Top Records set is reproducible.

If the retrieval step yields no document with a valid conductivity field, the system enters a fallback mode. For example, this occurs when a query has a property that is not present in the database. The system skips numeric ranking entirely and instructs the model to answer, if possible, using only the raw text in the retrieved documents. If the requested information is not available, the model reports it.

The final step is to generate a human-readable answer constrained to the selected documents. We used LLama3.1:8b \cite{grattafiori2024llama3} as the LLM, with \textit{temperature = 0.0} to minimize stochastic variation. 

The prompt is constructed in two parts. First, a structured context block enumerates the top-N entries. This includes their identifiers, chemical formulas, conductivities, temperatures, data quality tiers, and structural families. Second, an instruction block directs the model to list the entries in descending order of conductivity. The model then provides a brief scientific commentary (typically two to three sentences) on observed trends, such as which structural families dominate the highest conductivities. 

In fallback mode, a different prompt template is used: the model is given a small batch of retrieved documents without any ranking and is instructed either to summarize what the database contains about the user's query or to explicitly state that no relevant data was found.




\subsection{Results and Discussion}
This section evaluates the behavior of the retrieval-augmented interface when applied to the curated conductivity database, focusing on whether the language model produces responses that remain numerically consistent with the retrieved records and scientifically meaningful in the SSE context. Using a small set of representative domain queries, we assess the practical utility of this semantic query layer for interactive exploration of the matched dataset and document its prospects and limitations for interpreting database-specific fields and evidence-tier annotations.

To this end, we analyze three aspects of the queries: (i) the subset of entries retrieved by semantic similarity, (ii) the deterministically ranked "Top Records" derived from this subset, and (iii) the natural-language answer produced by the local language model. The retrieved and ranked records are directly compared with the underlying CSV, so that numerical correctness and coverage are evaluated independently of the model's generation. Taken together, the examples illustrate both the strengths of the interface, such as material's family-constrained discovery and rapid inspection of structurally equivalent entries, and its limitations, in particular with respect to approximate retrieval and imperfect enforcement of symbolic constraints, such as variables and chemical formulas. The generated queries and responses are fully accessible in the Supplementary Information and can be reproduced with the same dataset and model version.



\subsubsection{Q1: Family-constrained Discovery of Top-conductivity Garnets} \label{sec:q1}
In the first query, we asked for "the top five garnet-type solid electrolytes by ionic conductivity" and requested basic metadata (identifier, composition, MP ID, Tier, Family, Temperature). This is a typical exploratory task a domain user might perform when scanning a database for promising candidates within a structurally defined family, without having to remember column names or construct explicit filters. 

For this query, with $k = 100$, the semantic retrieval behaved as intended, returning results from the FAISS index that were exclusively labeled as "Garnet", showing that the documents and embedding model are sufficient to isolate a chemically coherent subset from the full database. Within this retrieved subset, the deterministic ranking procedure selected five distinct entries with the highest conductivity. 

Compared to the full set of garnet entries in the database, these five materials are all high-conductivity garnets. However, only 3 of 5 were in the strict global top 5 by conductivity. The other 2 entries were not present in the retrieved documents. This is expected when using a fixed-k nearest-neighbor search in embedding space. The query retrieves a local semantic neighborhood of garnets that match the question's phrasing, and deterministic ranking then selects the highest-conductivity entries within that group. Thus, this case shows a realistic usage pattern: the interface efficiently surfaces a small set of strong garnet candidates for further inspection but does not guarantee exhaustive coverage of all garnets in the database.

Despite this incomplete coverage and it missed 2 of 5 global top-5 entries, the generated answer identified trends such as \ce{Nb} and \ce{Ta} being promising doping agents for LLZO, highlighting well-known compositions such as \ce{Li5La3Nb2O12} (LLNO) \cite{Zhao2018DenseMethod} and \ce{Li5La3Ta2O12} (LLTa) \cite{Kotobuki2017ImprovementTa}, with conductivities on the order of $10^{-2}$ S cm$^{-1}$, as well as lower-conductivity variants of the LLZO. The model correctly treated missing temperature data (\textit{e.g.} for ACS-1664) as a limitation for quantitative comparisons, which aligns with best practice.

This reinforces the intended division of roles in the case study. The curated database and the deterministic ranking layer are treated as the source of truth after retrieval. The language model serves as a convenience layer for summarizing a small, grounded set of entries. In practice, the interface already provides useful functionality for database exploration. Users who require strict global optimality can still fall back to direct queries on the structured database.

\subsubsection{Q2: LLZO Cluster and Conductivity Spread at Fixed Structure}\label{sec:q2}
The second query focused on the garnet-type solid electrolyte \ce{Li7La3Zr2O12} (LLZO). The prompt asked to "identify entries that clearly correspond to LLZO", operationalized as records where the chemical formula is close to \ce{Li7La3Zr2O12}, small deviations in stoichiometry allowed, and/or where Family = Garnet and best\textunderscore mpid corresponds to LLZO (mp-942733). The prompt then requested a short list of entries, together with a summary of the conductivity ranges and Tiers, and a comment on the benefits of having them mapped to a single Materials Project structure.

With $k = 80$, the semantic retrieval step returned a chemically coherent neighborhood dominated by LLZO and LLZO-related garnets. The retrieved block contains numerous entries with the expected chemical formula and ID, covering conductivities from $10^{-5}$ S cm$^{-1}$ down to $10^{-11}$ S cm$^{-1}$ with several entries in the intermediate range between $10^{-6}$ S cm$^{-1}$ and $10^{-7}$ S cm$^{-1}$.

The same neighborhood also includes chemically similar Ta- and Nb-containing garnets, such as \ce{Li5La3Ta2O12} and a Te-garnet \ce{Li3Nd3Te2O12}. Given that the question explicitly allowed "small deviations in stoichiometry", this behavior is appropriate: the interface effectively expands the LLZO query into an LLZO-family cluster that matches common usage in the literature (Ta- and Nb-doped LLZO, \textit{etc.}).

The deterministic ranking by conductivity then operated as intended within this set. All values are taken directly from the curated dataset, and the RAG pipeline did not fabricate any compositions, MP IDs, or conductivity numbers. 

The LLM answer correctly highlights several of these features while also illustrating two important limitations. First, it reports that "the conductivity of LLZO ranges from approximately 1.660e-02 to 9.500e-07 S/cm, with most measurements falling in the range $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ S cm$^{-1}$", and lists a set of "Top Records for LLZO" that mixes Ta-garnets with strict LLZO entries, highlighting how the model is effectively treating the broader "LLZO-family" cluster. Second, when commenting on data quality, it states that "Tier values ... mostly T2 and T4 indicate a moderate to high level of confidence", which contradicts the project's definition (T1 is the highest confidence, T4 is the lowest). The Tier labels are correctly read from the retrieved rows, but their semantics are misinterpreted because the Tier scheme was not defined in the prompt.

Together, these observations reinforce three central points of the case study. First, the LLZO query showcases the main added value of the database itself: multiple heterogeneous experimental reports can be collapsed onto a single reference structure, making it straightforward to inspect the true multi-order-of-magnitude spread in the reported conductivities and their Tier annotations. Second, the semantic layer is useful: by allowing small stoichiometric deviations, the interface can pull in LLZO-related doped garnets that are often discussed together with LLZO in the literature, which is valuable for exploratory analysis. Third, project-specific categorical labels and naming conventions (such as the Tier scheme or the exact meaning of "LLZO") cannot be expected to be inferred by a language model without explicit definition. In practice, strict categorical distinctions are best enforced via structured database queries. The RAG interface should be understood as a tool for surfacing and narrating retrieved subsets, not for inferring the semantics of project-specific labels.

\subsubsection{Q3: Temperature Coverage}\label{sec:q3}


In the first two queries (Q1 and Q2), the interface had access to plenty of grounded numerical information in the retrieved documents: conductivities, compositions, MP IDs, and Tier labels were all present, and we observed no evidence that the model invented values outside the database. However, those examples did not test what happens when the model is asked to reason about a field that is essentially empty in the relevant part of the dataset. Q3 was therefore designed explicitly as a stress test in such a corner case: \textit{can the system avoid hallucination and remain grounded when there is effectively no temperature information to support the requested analysis?}

Prior examination of the full database showed that this situation occurs naturally for measurement temperature: when all entries are globally ranked by conductivity, the first records with non-missing temperature values appear only at positions 68 and 69. This means that none of the top 60 conductors has a recorded measurement temperature - in this last question, we exploit this fact.

We biased the query towards very high conductivity and set the retrieval parameter to $k = 60$, so that, by construction, the retrieved neighborhood would consist of genuine high-conductivity entries but should contain no valid temperature values (unlike queries such as "list entries with the highest measurement temperature"). Therefore, the goal was instead to probe behavior in the most challenging region, where the relevant field is systematically absent in the materials of interest, and to check whether, under these conditions, the model would hallucinate.

The retrieval and deterministic ranking behaved exactly as expected. The retrieved set and the "Top Records" block contained highly conductive materials that matched the top of the global distribution. For all these entries, the temperature field was marked as not available. This reflects a systematic pattern in the underlying literature: the most striking conductivity values are often reported without a clearly specified temperature, or in a form that cannot be captured as a single scalar metadata field. The database faithfully encodes this situation and does not attempt to infer or impute temperatures for such records.

The language model's answer did not attempt to patch this gap through fabrication. Faced with a retrieved set in which the requested field is effectively empty, the model neither hallucinated temperature values nor fabricated a temperature-based ranking. Instead, it explicitly remarked that the retrieved high-conductivity examples lack temperature values and concluded that a global temperature-centric comparison is not supported by the available data. In contrast to Q1 and Q2 - where the model had abundant grounded numbers and still had to be interpreted carefully - Q3 places the system in a situation where the only scientifically honest answer is essentially "there is not enough temperature metadata here to do what you asked", and the interface behaves conservatively rather than inventing details.

This query, therefore, serves as a deliberate scope and robustness test for the case study. The present pipeline is intentionally conductivity-centric: it treats temperature as optional contextual metadata and does not attempt to reconstruct missing measurement conditions. For analyses that genuinely require systematic temperature dependence, a different workflow would be needed, based on a pre-filtered subset with well-annotated temperatures and possibly additional manual curation. In the context of this work, Q3 makes that limitation explicit and, together with Q1 and Q2, shows that the retrieval-augmented interface remains grounded in the curated dataset both when it has rich numerical support and when it is confronted with a field that is systematically empty in the retrieved regime. This sets up the final discussion: the system is best understood as a semantic front-end for exploring the conductivity landscape defined by the database, while strict constraints and the interpretation of sparse metadata remain the responsibility of the structured layer and the user.



\section{Conclusion}

In this work we addressed a central bottleneck in data-driven solid-state electrolyte research: the lack of an explicit, machine-readable link between experimental ionic conductivity measurements and rigorously defined crystal structures. By harmonizing three open experimental resources (OBELiX, LiIon, and Shon \textit{et al.}) with the Materials Project, and by enforcing a transparent tiered matching scheme, we constructed a merged corpus in which every retained entry is associated with a unique MP identifier and a quantified level of structural confidence. This directly connects experimental performance data to the rich structural and computational descriptor space of a generalist materials database.

The resulting dataset comprises 3123 conductivity measurements mapped onto 310 distinct Materials Project entries, with 554 high-confidence records (T1–T3) and a larger, lower-confidence extension (T4). A systematic statistical analysis shows that the structurally assigned subset is dominated by the same families that underpin contemporary SSE research (garnet, NASICON-like, perovskite, LISICON/Thio-LISICON, argyrodite and related sulfide frameworks), while still including a chemically meaningful tail of halides, glasses/LIPON-type systems, thiophosphates, hydrides and other exploratory chemistries. The conductivity distributions span nearly twenty orders of magnitude and reproduce the expected hierarchy between oxide and sulfide frameworks, while the tier-resolved views confirm that the matching confidence primarily reflects structural evidence rather than selectively enriching or excluding particular conductivity regimes. These observations provide a strong internal sanity check on the curation pipeline and indicate that the merged dataset can support structure-aware analysis without introducing obvious artefacts in the conductivity landscape.

At the same time, the statistical overview makes visible several real limitations of the current experimental record. Temperature metadata are present only for a small fraction of entries and are heavily concentrated near room temperature, which constrains rigorous Arrhenius-type analyses even though basic trends in the sign of the temperature dependence are recovered. Per-material coverage is broad but shallow: most Materials Project entries are associated with only a handful of experimental measurements, with a small number of compositions forming a long right tail of heavily studied systems. Finally, a substantial fraction of the original literature lacks standardized structural-family labels. The merged dataset therefore does not ``fix'' these gaps; instead, it quantifies them and separates a high-confidence structural core from a broader exploratory regime, so that downstream users can make informed choices about which parts of the data are appropriate for a given task.

As a proof of concept, we also demonstrated that the curated, MP-linked corpus can support a lightweight retrieval-augmented interface that answers family- and composition-specific queries while keeping all numerical reasoning grounded in the underlying database. The case study illustrates both the strengths and the limits of this approach: semantic retrieval and deterministic ranking provide an efficient way to surface chemically coherent subsets and high-conductivity candidates, while model responses remain constrained by the actual content of the dataset, especially in regimes where key metadata (such as temperature) are sparse. In this sense, the language model functions as a convenience layer on top of a well-defined scientific object, rather than as a substitute for the structured database itself.

Overall, the present work provides more than a single merged dataset: it offers a concrete and reproducible blueprint for unifying experimental property databases with large-scale materials repositories. The combination of explicit structural linkage, tiered match confidence and quantified statistical coverage yields a resource that is immediately useful for high-throughput screening, benchmarking and machine-learning model development, while simultaneously highlighting where additional experimental and curation efforts are most urgently needed. The same workflow can, in principle, be extended to other properties and materials classes, providing a path toward increasingly dense, structurally anchored experimental datasets for materials discovery.
